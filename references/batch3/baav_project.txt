  Industry Overview

  The Indian automotive industry is seeing strong growth. It is becoming
  one of the most dynamic parts of the country’s manufacturing sector.
  This growth is driven by higher disposable incomes, urbanization, and
  changing consumer preferences. Over the last decade, the industry has
  expanded steadily. The market, which had about 3.4 million units in
  2020, is expected to reach nearly 8.4 million units by 2030. This
  reflects rising demand across all major vehicle categories.

  Passenger vehicles are the largest segment, boosted by better
  affordability, a wider range of models, and improved safety and
  technology features. At the same time, three-wheelers and light
  commercial vehicles are also gaining popularity due to their role in
  urban mobility and last-mile logistics.

  Commercial segments like heavy trucks and buses are likely to grow
  slowly as economic activity picks up and infrastructure development
  speeds up. The steady increase across all segments shows broad growth
  rather than isolated pockets of expansion. This rise is backed by
  improvements in manufacturing, local supply chains, and a shift toward
  cleaner mobility solutions. Overall, the Indian automotive sector is
  set for significant growth, offering many opportunities for OEMs,
  component manufacturers and new mobility companies over the next
  decade.

  Porter’s Five Forces Model

  1. Threat of New Entrants: MODERATE

  Traditional barriers still exist, but the shift to electric vehicles
  (EVs) is drawing new players.

  Capital & Regulation: High factory costs and strict safety and
  emission rules protect established companies like Toyota and VW.

  The "Tech" Loophole: The ease of making EVs allows tech companies like
  Xiaomi and Sony to team up with partners like Foxconn, letting them
  skip building their own factories.

  Service Barriers: New companies struggle to set up service networks,
  often called "service hell." However, over-the-air software updates
  are gradually easing this need.

  2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: HIGH

  Power is split between commodity suppliers and tech monopolies.

  Critical Tech Oligopoly: Suppliers for batteries, such as CATL, and
  chips, like Nvidia, hold significant power. Automakers can’t easily
  switch suppliers and must accept their terms.

  Commodity Suppliers: Suppliers of standard parts like tires and steel
  have little power due to many competitors and competitive bidding.

  Strategic Lock-in: Automakers are forming joint ventures (JVs) to
  secure parts. However, this can tie them to certain technologies and
  limit their options in supply chain disruptions.

  3. Bargaining Power of Buyers: HIGH

  Digital tools and economic factors give consumers more power.

  Transparency: Online price comparisons and direct-to-consumer sales,
  like those from Tesla, have removed dealers' ability to negotiate.

  Eroding Loyalty: Buyers now focus more on tech specs, such as range
  and screen size, than on brand loyalty. Switching brands now costs
  almost nothing.

  Price Sensitivity: High interest rates push Chinese manufacturers like
  BYD to price aggressively, forcing traditional brands to cut profit
  margins. Fleet buyers, such as Uber and Lyft, further push down prices
  with their buying power.

  4. Threat of Substitutes: HIGH

  Competition extends beyond other cars; it includes options other than
  owning a vehicle.

  Urban Mobility: In crowded cities, efficient public transit and
  congestion fees make owning a vehicle less attractive.

  Robotaxis: As self-driving technology improves, using a ride-hailing
  service, like Waymo, may become cheaper than owning a car, threatening
  sales.

  Micro-Mobility: Electric bikes and scooters are taking over short
  trips under five miles, often replacing second vehicles in homes.

  5. Competitive Rivalry: INTENSE

  This is the strongest force, marked by fierce competition in a crowded
  market.

  Zero-Sum Game: Growth in developed markets is flat; sales must come
  from competitors, leading to higher marketing costs.

  Price Wars: Tesla and Chinese manufacturers have begun aggressive
  price cuts, hurting profit margins across the industry.

  High Exit Barriers: Unions and government pressures keep struggling
  factories open, creating an oversupply situation.

  Tech Warfare: The focus has shifted from hardware, like engines, to
  software, creating a clash between traditional car companies and agile
  tech firms in Silicon Valley.[][]

  Corporate strategy

  TVS

  Global Expansion: TVS has built a robust export engine, with nearly
  25% of its revenue coming in from exports. The company dominates
  markets in Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia. A strategic
  partnership with Emil Frey allows entry into European markets without
  having to build up a new distribution network from scratch.

  Aggressive Premiumization: Acquiring Norton Motorcycles (£200M
  Investment) propels TVS into the global super-premium market.
  Similarly, the acquisition of Swiss E-Mobility Group (SEMG) gives
  immediate access to the high-margin e-bike market in the DACH region.

  "Smart Mobility" Transition: TVS has established a separate vertical
  for EVs to ensure agility and speed. Substantial investments in
  software, electronics, and digital connectivity are transforming the
  company from a traditional manufacturer to a smart mobility provider.

  Bajaj

  Global Expansion: A strategic shift to local manufacturing in Brazil
  with its Manaus plant allows Bajaj to bypass tariffs and capture the
  Latin American market. Exports have been shifted to resilient markets
  in LatAm and the Middle East, achieving record revenues.

  Strategic Alliances: Bajaj will assume majority control of KTM to lead
  operational turnarounds at the company and leverage its partnership
  with Triumph for a global play in the mid-weight premium segment.

  Subsidiary Structure: The company founded separate focused
  subsidiaries for EV innovation, Chetak Technology Ltd, and financing,
  Bajaj Auto Credit Ltd, to ensure focus and agility.

  Hero

  Aggressive Premiumization: A strategic partnership with
  Harley-Davidson allowed the co-development of the X440 and Hero's own
  Maverick 440, thus fast-tracking entry into the 400cc+ segment. Retail
  is being transformed with exclusive "Hero Premia" stores for top-tier
  products.

  Global Expansion: Under the 'Make in India for the World', Hero aims
  for 10% revenue from international markets. It has entered the
  Philippines and Brazil and is planning to launch EVs in the UK/Europe
  by mid-2025.

  Multi-pronged EV strategy: Hero adopts a hybrid approach-building its
  own brand Vida, hedging with a major investment in Ather Energy, and
  also partnering with Zero Motorcycles for high-performance electric
  bikes.

  Competitive strategy

  TVS

  How TVS Wins: It competes based on Product Differentiation and Quality
  Leadership. TVS uses feature-rich, high-quality product offerings to
  avoid low-price wars.

  "Power of Choice" - EV Portfolio: TVS provides an assortment of EVs.
  The iQube targets the mass-premium segment with a focus on reliability
  and familiarity, while the TVS X targets enthusiasts who crave high
  performance and showcases the brand's technological capabilities

  Differentiation through Technology: Early adoption of advanced
  features like the SmartConnect platform for navigation and analytics
  positions TVS as a technology leader that appeals more to a young
  audience..

  Service Network Leverage: Backed by a legacy network of more than
  4,000 touchpoints, TVS offers unmatched service assurance to State
  Bank of India customers and helps overcome "service anxiety" about
  digital-first competitors.

  Bajaj

  How Bajaj Wins: Bajaj competes based on Premiumization and
  Differentiation to avoid low-margin price wars.

  Premium Focus: The company is dominant in the high-margin 125cc+
  "sports" segment and has created a separate "Pro-Biking" vertical to
  provide a distinct retail experience for its premium brands.

  EV Strategy: Differing from competitors with gadget-focused bikes, the
  Chetak is being sold on reliability and durability ("metal body").
  Bajaj uses its extensive pan-India network of over 4,000 touchpoints
  to promise better after-sales service.

  Multi-fuel defense: Bajaj is defending its leadership in commercial
  vehicles with both CNG and Electric three-wheelers to offer a
  practical, cost-effective transition for the fleet operators.

  Hero

  ​How Hero Wins: Hero competes via Cost Leadership in the mass market
  and "Value Premium" in higher segments.

  Unmatched Distribution: Hero boasts the world's largest network of
  9,000+ touchpoints, superior rural reach, and accessibility of service
  as a huge barrier to entry against EV startups.

  Scale Advantage: As the volume leader, Hero uses economies of scale to
  price entry-level models like Splendor aggressively and remains
  dominant in price sensitive segments.

  Differentiation: Hero creates new categories with innovations like the
  Surge S32 (convertible 3-wheeler to 2-wheeler) and addresses high EV
  costs through Battery-as-a-Service models.

PESTEL ANALYSIS

  The PESTEL framework analyses the macro-environmental factors
  influencing an industry, offering insights into opportunities and
  challenges. It examines Political, Economic, Social, Technological,
  Environmental, and Legal aspects that shape industry dynamics.
  For the Indian Automobile Industry, PESTEL highlights key drivers such
  as government policies, technological advancements, rising consumer
  expectations, and sustainability pressures. Together, these factors
  define the growth trajectory and competitive environment of the
  sector.

Political Factors

  Government Policies:
  The Indian government plays an active role in shaping the sector
  through initiatives such as the FAME-II scheme, which incentivizes the
  adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) via subsidies and reduced GST.
  Policies supporting hybrid vehicles, battery manufacturing, and
  mobility electrification continue to influence manufacturer
  strategies.

  Emission and Safety Regulations:
  Regulations like Bharat Stage VI (BSVI) norms and mandatory safety
  features (airbags, ABS, crash test compliance) have increased
  technological requirements and manufacturing costs. However, these
  have improved overall product quality and pushed companies toward
  cleaner technologies.

  Manufacturing Incentives:
  Policies under Make in India, PLI schemes, and localization mandates
  aim to enhance domestic production, reduce reliance on imports, and
  boost job creation. This encourages automakers to set up local
  manufacturing and component sourcing networks.

  Geopolitical Influence:
  Tensions with countries that supply critical inputs (e.g., China for
  batteries, semiconductors) impact supply chains. Changes in trade
  agreements, import tariffs, and international relations can affect
  availability and cost of key parts.

Economic Factors

  Rising Disposable Income:
  India’s expanding middle class continues to drive demand for
  two-wheelers and passenger vehicles. Increased urban employment and
  income growth support sustained industry expansion.

  Inflation & Interest Rates:
  Volatile fuel prices, inflation, and rising interest rates directly
  influence consumer purchasing decisions. Higher EMIs and operating
  costs may delay purchases or shift demand toward fuel-efficient and
  electric models.

  Supply Chain Costs & Disruptions:
  Global shortages of semiconductors, fluctuations in raw material
  prices (steel, aluminium, lithium), and shipping delays increase
  production costs and extend delivery wait times.

  Export Opportunities:
  India is developing as a manufacturing hub for small cars and
  two-wheelers. Growth in exports to Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin
  America provides diversification and revenue stability.

Social Factors

  Shift in Consumer Preferences:
  Consumers are increasingly prioritizing sustainability, fuel
  efficiency, and safety features. Digital additions like connected
  tech, navigation, and infotainment are now expected in even mid-range
  vehicles.

  Urbanization:
  Rapid urban growth boosts demand for compact cars and two-wheelers
  suited to congested environments. This supports high sales for
  commuter motorcycles and small family cars.

  Affordability as Key Driver:
  India remains one of the world’s most price-sensitive automobile
  markets. This constrains aggressive pricing and makes efficiencies
  critical for competitiveness.

  Rural Market Growth:
  The rural economy has shown increasing purchasing power, making it an
  important market for entry-level motorcycles, scooters, and low-cost
  commuter cars.

Technological Factors

  EV and Battery Technology:
  Manufacturers are investing heavily in electric drivetrains, battery
  innovation, and hybrid systems. Advances in battery range, charging
  speed, and durability are reshaping product development.

  Digital & Connected Technologies:
  Features like telematics, connectivity, AI-based diagnostics, ADAS,
  and IoT-enabled features are increasingly included across segments.
  This pushes firms to invest in advanced electronics and software
  capabilities.

  Automation & Smart Manufacturing:
  The industry is adopting robotics, automation, and data-driven
  manufacturing to reduce costs, improve quality, and enhance
  efficiency.

  Emerging Mobility Models:
  Growth in shared mobility, subscription-based ownership, and digital
  retail platforms is transforming traditional dealership-driven sales
  models.

Environmental Factors

  Sustainability Focus:
  Growing concerns about pollution and climate change drive consumers
  and regulators toward clean mobility solutions such as EVs, hybrids,
  and CNG vehicles.

  Resource Scarcity:
  Dependence on imported lithium, cobalt, and other rare metals affects
  EV affordability and supply stability. Battery recycling and circular
  economy initiatives are gaining importance.

  Air Pollution Concerns:
  Major Indian cities exceed global pollution norms, prompting stricter
  emission laws and public pushes for cleaner transport solutions.

  Green Manufacturing:
  Automakers increasingly adopt renewable energy sources and
  eco-friendly processes to reduce carbon footprints across production
  facilities.

Legal Factors

  Taxation Structure:
  ICE vehicles attract higher GST (28% + cess), while EVs enjoy a
  concessional 5% GST rate. This price differential strongly impacts
  consumer choice and industry pricing strategies.

  Safety Regulations:
  Mandatory features — including dual airbags, ABS, rear parking
  sensors, and crash testing — have increased manufacturing costs but
  improved product safety across the market.

  Intellectual Property & Technology Laws:
  Stronger IP laws encourage global players to bring advanced
  technologies to India while protecting proprietary EV and software
  innovations.

  Labour Compliance:
  Manufacturers must adhere to strict labour laws regarding wages,
  worker safety, and unionisation. These influence productivity, costs,
  and plant operations.

SWOT ANALYSIS

  A SWOT analysis examines the Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and
  Threats of a company, offering a structured framework to evaluate
  internal capabilities and external industry pressures. For Hero
  MotoCorp, Bajaj Auto, and TVS Motor, this analysis highlights their
  distinct competitive positions within the Indian two-wheeler industry,
  revealing key advantages, strategic gaps, and areas requiring
  attention. It also helps identify emerging growth avenues such as
  electrification, premiumisation, and global expansion while assessing
  risks from competition, regulatory changes, and shifting consumer
  preferences.

 Strengths

Key Inputs and Assumptions:

1.  Revenue & Growth Assumptions:
      Historical financial performance over multiple years was analysed
      to estimate long-term growth trends.
      The intrinsic growth rate was derived using the fundamental
      relationship: g = ROIC × Reinvestment Rate. Average historical
      ROIC and reinvestment metrics were used to capture sustainable
      firm-level growth. This method ensures that projected growth is
      consistent with the firm’s ability to reinvest and generate
      returns on capital.

2.  Reinvestment Rate:
      Reinvestment rates were calculated using:
      Reinvestment Rate = (Net Capex+ΔWC)/EBIT(1−T). Since reinvestment
      can fluctuate due to working-capital releases or temporary capex
      reductions, abnormal negative values were normalised to avoid
      overstating FCFF. A stabilised reinvestment assumption aligned
      with long-term expected growth was used for forward projections.

3.  ROIC (Return on Invested Capital):
      ROIC was computed using NOPAT and invested capital based on
      operating assets. ROIC = EBIT(1−T)/Invested Capital. A multi-year
      average ROIC was applied for forecasting, as ROIC reflects the
      fundamental efficiency with which the firm generates returns on
      its deployed capital (both debt and equity).

4.  Cost of Capital (WACC):
      The discount rate for FCFF valuation was derived using the
      Weighted Average Cost of Capital formula:
      WACC = we ⋅ ke + wd ⋅ kd(1−T)

  Cost of Equity was estimated using CAPM with an adjusted levered beta.

  Cost of Debt was derived from the firm’s borrowing profile or
  synthetic credit rating–based spreads.

  Capital structure weights were based on current market values of
  equity and debt.
  The resulting WACC represents the opportunity cost of capital for both
  debt and equity holders.

5.  Terminal Growth Rate:
      The terminal growth rate applied in the perpetuity calculation
      reflects long-term industry expectations and macroeconomic growth
      assumptions.
