EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Overview of the Renewable Energy Industry

The renewable energy sector is one of the world's fastest-growing industries, driven by climate
commitments, energy security concerns, and rapidly falling technology costs. Solar and wind
have become the default choice for new capacity addition globally, complemented by emerging
technologies such as battery storage and green hydrogen.

Renewable Energy in India

India has emerged as a global leader in clean energy, ranking 4th worldwide in total renewable
energy installed capacity. Total installed renewable capacity reached 220.10 GW as of March
2025, adding a record 29.52 GW in FY 2024–25. Solar leads at 105.65 GW, while wind has
crossed 50 GW. India's flagship target is 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030, and the
country crossed 50% of installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by July 2025, which
is FIVE years ahead of its Paris Agreement commitment.

Key Trends

India's renewable growth is underpinned by strong government policy, including the PLI scheme
for domestic solar manufacturing, PM Surya Ghar, ISTS charge waivers for renewable projects
until 2028, and mandatory Renewable Purchase Obligations. The market is also maturing
structurally: hybrid solar-wind projects with battery storage now dominate auction volumes,
enabling firm dispatchable renewable energy. The Commercial & Industrial segment is a
fast-emerging demand driver, projected to require 78 GW of renewable energy by FY30.

Key Challenges

Grid infrastructure and transmission bottlenecks continue to delay project commissioning.
DISCOM financial stress creates payment risk for power producers. Land acquisition remains
operationally complex, and competitive tariff auctions have compressed developer margins
significantly. Dependence on imported critical minerals for battery storage also poses supply
chain risk.

hybrid, storage-integrated solutions; the frontier where JSW Energy, NHPC, and Suzlon Energy
are each actively investing and differentiating.

PORTER'S FIVE FORCES MODEL


1. Threat of New Entrants - LOW TO MODERATE

Barriers to entry in renewable energy are substantial. Projects require enormous upfront
capital. For example, NHPC alone is investing ₹98,107 crore across eight projects, thereby
making funding access a critical differentiator. Regulatory complexity, including environmental
clearances, land acquisition, grid connectivity norms, and ALMM compliance, adds further
friction. Competitive tariff auctions reward scale and low-cost capital, disadvantaged smaller or
newer entrants. Incumbents also benefit from deep-rooted relationships with state utilities,
established supply chains, and decades of execution track record.

However, well-capitalized conglomerates such as Adani Green, Reliance New Energy, and
global sovereign-backed funds continue to enter the sector, moderating this protection. Overall:
Low to Moderate threat.




                                                 5
2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers - MODERATE

Solar panel supply was historically dominated by Chinese manufacturers, but India's PLI
scheme and ALMM framework have driven domestic module manufacturing capacity to 144 GW
per annum by 2025, reducing this dependence. In wind, the supply market is more
concentrated. For example, Suzlon's vertically integrated model (blades, nacelles, hubs, towers
all in-house) insulates it from supplier leverage, while non-integrated developers remain
exposed. EPC contractors exist in a competitive market, limiting their bargaining power. The key
area of concern is critical minerals (lithium, cobalt) for battery storage, where global supply
is concentrated, giving these suppliers relatively higher power. Overall: Moderate.




                                                6
3. Bargaining Power of Buyers - MODERATE TO HIGH
Government bodies such as SECI, NTPC, NHPC, state DISCOMs, all procure power through
reverse auctions, driving solar tariffs to record lows of ₹2.51/kWh. Once a 25-year PPA is
signed, sellers have minimal pricing flexibility while buyers enjoy long-term cost certainty. For
Suzlon, C&I clients (54% of its order book) bring more negotiating balance but are still
price-conscious. NHPC's government ownership partially shelters it from pure buyer pressure
through regulated tariff arrangements. Overall: Moderate to High.



4. Threat of Substitutes - LOW TO MODERATE
Thermal coal power remains the primary substitute, but India's policy trajectory strongly favors
renewables, and no new coal capacity additions are planned beyond the current 50 GW
pipeline. Natural gas is constrained by infrastructure limitations and price volatility. Nuclear
power carries prohibitive costs and long lead times. Green hydrogen, rather than substituting
renewables, increases electricity demand which is a net positive for the sector. The key
intra-sector substitute risk is solar vs. wind competing for the same projects and capital, a
tension being resolved by the rapid rise of hybrid configurations. Overall: Low to Moderate.




                                                  7
5. Industry Rivalry Among Existing Competitors - HIGH

Rivalry is intense across all segments. In utility-scale generation, JSW Energy, Adani Green,
Tata Power, NTPC Green, and government PSUs all compete for the same auction lots, with
price as the primary battleground. Margins are thin and execution capability separates winners
from losers. In wind turbine manufacturing, Suzlon faces competition from Inox Wind
domestically and from Envision and Vestas internationally, though Suzlon's ~30% domestic
market share and end-to-end manufacturing capability provide a durable moat. NHPC faces
competition in hydropower from SJVN and other PSUs but benefits from government-allocated
project sites and regulatory protection. Companies are differentiating through technology like
BESS, green hydrogen, higher-hub-height turbines, and pumped storage to move beyond pure
price competition. Overall: High.

NET WORKING CAPITAL




Formula: Current Assets − Current Liabilities

Observations

   ●​ NHPC's net working capital declining from ~₹2,400 crore to near-zero or negative
      by FY25 reflects growing current liabilities (contractor payables, short-term
      borrowings for projects) outpacing current assets.
   ●​ Suzlon's NWC rising sharply from ~₹1,000 crore to ~₹3,200 crore is a direct
      consequence of its order book explosion - large customer advances inflow as orders
      are booked, boosting current assets dramatically.
   ●​ JSW's NWC turning slightly negative in FY22 before recovering reflects a period of
      aggressive short-term borrowing for merchant power trading and fuel procurement.

Suggestions




                                            24
   ●​ NHPC's declining NWC needs attention - if project-related payables continue to
      grow without corresponding revenue recognition, liquidity stress could emerge at
      the project level.
   ●​ Suzlon's rising NWC is a healthy sign but must be managed carefully - customer
      advances are liabilities until deliveries are made, and any project execution delays
      will convert NWC from an asset to a risk.
   ●​ JSW should improve NWC management by negotiating longer payment terms with
      fuel suppliers and shorter collection cycles from state utilities.

Limitation

   ●​ NWC figures in absolute rupee terms are not comparable across these three
      companies because of dramatically different revenue scales (Suzlon ~₹10,851 crore
      revenue in FY25 vs NHPC ~₹8,400 crore vs JSW ~₹11,700 crore).
   ●​ For NHPC, NWC is distorted by the treatment of government grants and project
      advances in current liabilities, which are not genuine short-term obligations.
   ●​ Suzlon's NWC surge is partly advance-driven - it does not represent organic
      working capital creation and can reverse sharply if order cancellations occur.

CASH RATIO




Formula: Cash & Cash Equivalents ÷ Current Liabilities

Observations

   ●​ JSW's cash ratio rising from ~0.25 to ~0.65 and then settling at ~0.52 reflects its
      growing cash pile from equity raises and operating cash flows, even as it deploys
      capital aggressively.




                                              28
   ●​ NHPC's stable cash ratio of ~0.20 is consistent with a government utility that
      regularly pays dividends and uses cash for project construction rather than holding
      it idle.
   ●​ Suzlon's near-zero cash ratio throughout (rising only slightly to ~0.15) reflects its
      past cash-strapped state, with most liquidity going toward debt repayment - by
      FY25, cash is being channeled into working capital for the order book surge.

Suggestions

   ●​ JSW's relatively high cash ratio is actually a positive buffer given its aggressive
      expansion - it signals that equity raises have been well-timed and the company is
      not yet cash-starved.
   ●​ NHPC should consider raising its cash ratio slightly as large projects (Subansiri,

   ●​ Suzlon needs to build cash reserves as order intake grows - executing a 5.5 GW
      order book requires significant upfront working capital that cannot be funded by
      cash flow alone.

Limitation

   ●​ Cash ratio is an extremely conservative and rarely actionable metric - holding high
      cash is itself a cost (opportunity cost of capital), so a low cash ratio is not necessarily
      bad if credit lines are available.
   ●​ NHPC has access to government-backed financing at concessional rates, meaning
      its cash ratio understates its true short-term access to funds.
   ●​ Suzlon's apparent low cash ratio does not capture its access to customer advances
      and working capital lines from banks, which are its true short-term liquidity
      sources.

3.1 Data and Methodology
Estimating beta is the first quantitative step in this valuation framework. It grounds the
discount rate in market-observed risk rather than assumption. Monthly stock return data for
JSW Energy, NHPC, and Suzlon Energy, along with Nifty 50 index returns, were compiled across
a 60-month window from April 2020 to March 2025, covering FY2021 through FY2025. Returns
are computed as simple percentage price changes using NSE end-of-month closing prices
sourced from Screener.in and Tijori Finance.


Monthly data is preferred over daily returns because it substantially reduces noise from
intraday liquidity effects and short-term market microstructure quirks, neither of which
reflects the genuine co-movement between a stock and the broader economy that beta is
designed to capture. A 60-month window is the practitioner standard: large enough to be
statistically meaningful, recent enough to capture the current business cycle.




3.2 Beta Computation: Dual-Method Verification
Two algebraically equivalent methods were applied to compute raw beta, providing a built-in
internal consistency check:


 B = SLOPE(R_stock, R_nifty)                       Regression slope method: Excel SLOPE function



 B = COVAR(R_stock, R_nifty) / VAR(R_nifty)        Covariance decomposition: algebraically identical



Both methods converge to near-identical values for all three companies, with only immaterial
decimal-level differences arising from floating-point precision. This confirms that the
underlying return data and formula linkages are consistent throughout the model.

3.4 Blume's Adjustment: Forward-Looking Beta
Raw regression betas are backward-looking by construction. Blume (1971) documented a
well-replicated empirical regularity: betas tend to mean-revert toward 1.0 over time as
companies mature and their risk profiles converge toward the market average. Applying a
purely historical beta as a forward-looking cost-of-capital input therefore introduces a
systematic bias. The standard correction weights the empirical estimate against the theoretical
market average:


 Adjusted B = 0.67 x Raw B + 0.33 x 1.0                 Blume (1971) mean-reversion adjustment



This moderates extreme values in both directions, pulling high betas slightly down and low
betas slightly up, producing a more conservative and empirically grounded forward estimate.




3.5 Unlevering and Re-Levering: Isolating Business Risk
A regression beta blends two distinct components: business risk (the inherent volatility of
operations) and financial risk (the amplification caused by debt in the capital structure). To
make meaningful cross-firm comparisons of fundamental risk, we first strip financial risk out




                                                   45
using the Hamada equation, and then reintroduce it at the target capital structure before
plugging the result into CAPM:


 B_unlevered = B_adjusted / [1 + (D/E)_book x          Remove financial risk: uses book-value D/E
 (1-t)]



 B_re-levered = B_unlevered x [1 + (D/E)_market        Restore at market-value D/E for CAPM
 x (1-t)]



Book D/E is used for unlevering because it reflects the capital structure over which the beta
was actually observed. Market D/E is used for re-levering because it reflects the leverage that
equity investors face today, the correct input for a forward-looking discount rate.

5. DCF VALUATION: FCFF MODEL

5.1 Methodological Refinement: Normalised NOPAT as Base FCFF
This iteration of the model introduces a significant and well-reasoned methodological
refinement: the base FCFF for all three companies is set to NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After
Tax = EBIT x (1 minus t)), rather than the mechanically computed FCFF that includes actual
capital expenditure. This is not an ad hoc assumption. It reflects a deliberate analytical choice
grounded in the economics of infrastructure valuation.


The actual FY2025 FCFFs for JSW Energy and NHPC are deeply negative, because both
companies are deploying capex that is 2 to 3 times their EBITDA to build assets with 25 to 30
year useful lives. Applying the Gordon Growth Model to a deeply negative starting FCFF would
generate a mathematically valid but economically meaningless negative terminal value and
negative enterprise value, not because the companies are worthless, but because the model
cannot capture the value of assets currently under construction.


The normalised NOPAT base resolves this by treating the business as if it were in a steady state
where maintenance capex equals depreciation, meaning net reinvestment is zero. This is a
recognised practitioner approach for capital-intensive businesses where the growth capex in
the explicit forecast period is reflected through the growing FCFF projections, while the base
anchors the model to current operating earnings power.


 Base FCFF = NOPAT = EBIT x (1-t)                    Maintenance Capex = D&A assumed; net
                                                     reinvestment = 0



Suzlon's base FCFF of Rs 908 crore similarly uses NOPAT rather than the actual FY2025 FCFF of
Rs -91 crore, the latter being negative primarily due to large NWC build-up from rapid order
book expansion, which is a growth-phase working capital effect rather than a structural
earnings problem.

6. RELATIVE VALUATION: EV/EBITDA AND P/E

6.1 The Role of Multiples in This Analysis
Given that the FCFF DCF still produces intrinsic values well below market prices for NHPC and
Suzlon, relative valuation multiples serve as the essential market reality check. They tell us what
investors are actually paying for comparable cash flows and growth profiles today, and whether
the premiums to DCF value are consistent with sector norms or represent genuine mispricing.


Two multiples are used: EV/EBITDA and P/E. EV/EBITDA is capital-structure-neutral and
strips out non-cash depreciation, making it the superior metric for comparing utilities with
different leverage and asset lives. P/E connects to headline earnings and is the most widely
cited benchmark by retail and institutional investors alike, but must be interpreted carefully
when earnings are in a rapid growth or turnaround phase.

6.5 Triangulation: Why No Single Method Tells the Full Story
The methodological journey across this report illustrates a core principle of equity valuation: no
single tool is sufficient, and the most dangerous mistake is to treat any one output as the
answer rather than as one piece of evidence.


The FCFF DCF (normalised) provides the theoretical anchor. It tells us what the business earns
in steady state and what that is worth at the required discount rate. Its limitation is that it
cannot capture growth investments not yet reflected in current NOPAT.


EV/EBITDA provides the market cross-check. It anchors value to what comparable businesses
trade at today, and it surfaces the CWIP discount for NHPC and the growth premium for Suzlon
far more clearly than the DCF does.


P/E connects to the earnings story. For JSW Energy and NHPC, it provides a clean read on how
the market is pricing earnings quality. For Suzlon, the forward P/E is the more relevant lens,
given the recency of full profitability.




                                                 59
Capacity-based valuation (Rs per MW of installed and pipeline capacity) provides the fourth leg
for JSW Energy and NHPC, where the infrastructure asset base is the primary store of value.


The renewable energy sector's structural tailwinds, India's 500 GW target by 2030, falling LCOE
curves, PLI schemes, and ESG-driven fund flows, create a valuation environment where
long-duration growth is being priced in well ahead of cash flow realisation. In that context, DCF
models anchored to current earnings will consistently appear to understate market values for
some companies while correctly identifying undervaluation in others. The analyst's job is to
understand the gap, explain it, and judge whether the market's implied growth assumptions are
reasonable.
