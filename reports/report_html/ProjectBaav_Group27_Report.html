Executive summary

Real estate is the sector where accounting profit and economic value drift furthest apart. Revenue is booked years after a flat is sold, land sits on the balance sheet at what it cost decades ago, and much of the profit of the largest developer arrives as a one-line share of a joint venture. We set out to separate what these three companies earn from what their share prices assume, using every framework in the course.

We worked in six steps, and the report follows them. We first studied the businesses and twenty years of the sector's history (section 2). We then read the accounts critically and rebuilt them so that operating profit, joint-venture income and one-off gains sit apart (sections 3–4), and measured performance on the cleaned numbers (section 5). We set the hurdle rate two defensible ways (section 6), valued each company with a cash-flow model, peer multiples and the brokers' net-asset-value method (sections 7–9), and finally tested what we found against market history and the companies' own disclosures (section 10). Our view

1. Godrej Properties: the cheapest of the three relative to peers and the only one the practitioner method values above price; the risk is that land buying keeps statutory cash flow negative.

2. DLF: fairly priced against peers with the strongest balance sheet, but only a long-run growth view justifies the price.

3. Phoenix Mills: a high-quality annuity priced like one that will compound at the rate of the economy for ever.

2The sector: two decades of boom and bust

Module 2 · strategy and competitive analysis · real-estate cycle

Before valuing anything we needed to understand how these businesses make money and how the sector behaves over a full cycle. We collected twenty years of prices and industry data and applied the strategy frameworks from the course.

The record is humbling. DLF listed in July 2007 near the top of the last boom; nineteen years later its dividend-adjusted return is 1.6% a year and the stock is still 23% below its December 2007 peak, after falling 91%. Phoenix fell 90% in 2008, took until 2017 to recover, and has compounded at 14.1% a year since listing. The Nifty Realty index has returned 3.8% a year since 2010 with a 74% drawdown, against 8.3% for the Nifty 50. Anyone valuing these stocks off the last three years of data is looking at one half of a cycle.

Physical prices behave very differently. BIS data show Indian house prices rising 9.4% a year in nominal terms since 2009 but only 3.5% in real terms; in the latest quarter real prices were -0.3% year on year. The correlation between annual changes in house prices and in the realty index is -0.25: equities move on expectations, rates and bookings momentum, and overshoot the physical market in both directions. Hypothesis H5 is rejected.

Where are we now? The latest data read as late-cycle. Anarock's top-seven-city sales rose only 3% in Q3 2026 after falling 6% in Q2 (PropEquity's wider count fell 6%), while launches rose 18% and unsold inventory 12% to about 6.3 lakh units; prices are still up about 7%. On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% and moved to 'calibrated tightening', and the 10-year G-Sec is near multi-year highs. Supply outrunning sales, and rates turning up, is the combination that preceded past corrections in Figure 1.

Customer segments differ sharply. DLF sells to high-net-worth buyers and non-resident Indians for whom a Gurugram apartment is partly an investment; Godrej sells to salaried, mortgage-financed buyers across cities, so its demand is rate-sensitive; Phoenix's customers are tenants whose sales depend on urban discretionary spending. Customer lifetime value is highest for Phoenix (tenants renew leases repeatedly) and lowest for developers, whose buyers rarely buy twice.

The industry in numbers, and where our three companies stand

Housing volumes in the top seven cities have grown only 1.3% a year since the 2014 peak: sales fell 60% to the 2020 trough, launches fell by three-quarters from 2014 to 2017 as demand, then credit, dried up, and unsold stock fell from 8.0 lakh units (2016) to about 5.5 lakh. In NCR, DLF's home market, unsold stock fell from 88 months of sales in 2020 to 17. In 2025 volumes fell 14% while value rose 6%. Buyers are moving to costlier homes, while the RBI's house-price index rose only 2–4% a year. Delivery risk is industry-wide: about 6.3 lakh homes worth ₹5 lakh crore are stuck or delayed (1.6 years of national sales), and only 26% of projects registered with MahaRERA are complete. Against that backdrop our firms are taking share: Godrej's bookings rose 16% in FY26 against 6% for the market, lifting its (indicative) share of top-seven sales value from 5.2% to 5.7%; DLF's dipped to 3.4% as it deferred launches. Godrej delivered 121–123% of its delivery guidance in FY25–26; Phoenix opened its three 2023 malls within 0–4 months of guidance and grows rent at roughly twice the Grade-A mall market (5–8%), while a fifth of India's malls are near-empty. Offices had a record 2025 (gross leasing 83–89 msf), the demand behind DCCDL's 98% occupancy.

6Cost of capital

Module 6 · risk-free rate, ERP, CRP; regression, bottom-up and industry beta; Kd; WACC; multi-factor check

A valuation is only as good as its discount rate, and this is where analysts disagree most. We estimated each input several ways and carried two defensible versions forward instead of choosing one.

Risk-free rate. The 10-year G-Sec yields 7.38%. It contains India's sovereign default spread (1.87%), which we remove to get a rupee risk-free rate of 5.51%. Equity risk premium. Damodaran's implied mature-market premium (4.23%) plus India's country risk premium (2.85%) gives 7.08%. History agrees, loosely: over 2005–2026 the Sensex beat the G-Sec by 5.6% a year geometrically, but with a standard error of 5.8% . That is too noisy to estimate a premium from, so we use the implied figure.

Regression beta. DLF 1.70 (SE 0.18, R² 0.60), Godrej 2.22 (SE 0.26), Phoenix 1.09 (SE 0.22). Godrej's 95% interval of 1.69–2.75 alone moves its cost of equity by about seven points.

Beta is not a constant (H3). Phoenix's rolling beta has ranged from 0.48 to 1.91, Godrej's from 0.70 to 2.42. A single five-year regression is one draw from a moving target. Bottom-up beta. Unlevering 12 large developers at market D/E and taking the median gives 1.31, relevered to 1.32 for DLF and 1.64 for Godrej. For Phoenix, a rental business, developer betas overstate risk; listed REITs have betas of 0.1–0.4 with R² below 12%, so we use Phoenix's own Blume-adjusted beta (1.06).

Industry lens. Damodaran's India dataset gives an unlevered beta of 0.69 for 156 developers and 0.24 for real-estate operators. These averages are dragged down by thinly traded small caps, whose prices react to the market with a lag (H2). We carry them as lens B rather than discard them, because they show how sensitive the answer is. A Dimson (1979) test confirms the bias: adding last month's market return raises the median peer beta from 1.47 to 1.59, and lifts small caps such as Puravankara (1.69 → 2.43) and Anant Raj (1.66 → 2.27) far more than DLF (1.70 → 1.72). For Phoenix, lens B implies a cost of equity of 7.3% — below the G-Sec — so we treat it as an upper bound.

A two-factor test of interest-rate sensitivity is in Appendix B; rate risk turned out to be real but statistically weak (H4).

7Intrinsic valuation

Module 7 · growth estimation, FCFF, FCFE, APV, EVA, residual income, DDM, real options, scenarios

With cash flows and discount rates in hand, we built a ten-year DCF for each company, then ran it backwards to ask what the share price assumes. Scenarios and a simulation test how far the answer moves.

Growth. Historical revenue growth was 11.3% (DLF), 32.4% (Godrej) and 18.4% (Phoenix). Fundamental growth (reinvestment rate × ROIC) fails for developers. DLF's reinvestment is negative because advances shrink its capital, and Godrej's ROIC is near zero, so growth comes from sales already made but not yet recognised, visible in customer advances, and reinvestment is tied to growth through a sales-to-capital ratio.

Reality check. We tested every FY27 assumption against the two FY27 quarters already reported. DLF's revenue fell 37% year on year in H1 FY27 as recognition paused between project completions, so we cut its FY27 growth from +40% to −15% and moved the step-up to FY28–30, when Privana and the Dahlias complete; the new path needs only about 7% growth in H2. Godrej's H1 revenue more than doubled and Phoenix's rose 14%, both consistent with our assumptions (Quarterly sheet).

How aggressive is that? Against a naive linear trend of FY23–26 revenue (Excel FORECAST, the method earlier cohorts used), our FY31 revenue is 1.6x the trend for DLF, 2.3x for Godrej and 1.1x for Phoenix. The developers' multiples are deliberate: four years of completion-based revenue say little about sales already contracted, which customer advances (2.9x and 7.8x revenue) show are far larger. Even this generous path leaves value below price, so the conclusion does not rest on a cautious forecast.

The same cash flows valued through EVA (invested capital plus PV of EVA) reproduce the FCFF value to the rupee (difference 0); APV confirms debt adds little, since DLF is net cash and Godrej's tax shield is offset by distress risk.

What the price implies

Sensitivity grids move DLF between ₹250 and ₹370 and no sensible combination reaches the price. Solved backwards, the price needs a target margin of 131% for DLF and 196% for Phoenix (impossible), or a uniform discount rate of 9.8%, 12.2% and 7.7%, or, holding our discount rates, perpetual growth of 12.1%, 9.4% and 11.6%. The third is the most economically meaningful: investors are treating these firms as perpetual claims on Indian nominal GDP. That is not irrational for an economy the IMF expects to grow 7% in real and about 10% in nominal terms in FY27 (nominal growth slowed to 8–8.5% in FY26 on low inflation), but it abandons the textbook discipline that terminal growth should not exceed the risk-free rate, and it leaves little margin of safety: the spread between the stable discount rate and the implied growth is only 0.5, 1.8 and 0.5 percentage points (H1).

Analysts in the industry value developers differently, so we rebuilt their net-asset-value method from company disclosures, read the companies' own earnings calls and filings, and compared our numbers with broker targets.

The market trades on sales bookings, collections, launch pipelines and rental growth, and the sell-side values developers on net asset value. Verification. Our profit lines match the companies (Godrej ₹1,850 cr and Phoenix ₹1,224 cr exactly; DLF within 0.2%). Revenue and cash differ by definition: our DLF revenue is 4.3% below 'revenue from operations' as reported, and our DLF net cash excludes ₹5,003 cr of RERA-restricted cash. DLF's share of JV profit in our data (₹1,793 cr) is within 1% of 66.66% of DCCDL's reported profit, confirming that line is essentially DCCDL. Against DLF's audited FY26 annual report, our equity, total assets, inventory, share of JV profit and operating cash flow match to the rupee, and the RERA balances in its notes (about ₹11,170 cr) confirm the escrow figure management gave on its call. Godrej's audited FY26 statements tie equally exactly (total assets ₹81,894 cr, inventory ₹57,807 cr, profit ₹1,841 cr, operating cash flow −₹2,003 cr); its other income of ₹3,279 cr is 39% of total income.

DLF. At the 7.5% cap rate a leading broker uses, DCCDL is worth ₹85,389 cr gross and DLF's stake ₹44,821 cr after DCCDL's debt, more than twice the ₹20,291 cr we get by capitalising DLF's share of DCCDL's after-tax profit at our cost of equity. The gap matters: a 7.5% cap rate on EBITDA embeds a far lower required return than CAPM gives. Adding five years of pipeline margin at guided sales (₹18,206 cr) and net cash gives identifiable NAV of ₹312 a share, close to our DCF (₹312–386 if all of the ₹24,500 cr of gross margin management says is still to be recognised on sold units were added; part of it already sits in net cash). The market's remaining ₹93,997 cr is, by elimination, the value placed on land beyond the visible pipeline: about ₹8,393 per sq ft of the 112 million sq ft of potential outside the launch pipeline (137 msf balance potential per management, less ~25 msf in the pipeline), against a book value of about ₹1,804 per sq ft of inventory and land (H7). This puts a rupee figure on the real-option argument.

Phoenix. Retail EBITDA at a 7% cap rate, offices at 8%, hotels at 12x EBITDA and work-in-progress at cost give a NAV to owners of ₹916 a share; the price is 114% above it. A 7% cap rate at the 11.75% discount rate an Indian REIT valuer uses implies perpetual rent growth of 4.75%, so practitioners' own conventions sit close to our 5.5% terminal growth. What closes the gap is an explicit growth premium; one broker adds 70% to its NAV.

Godrej. Brokers value Godrej on the profit already embedded in its sales. Twelve times FY28 embedded profit (bookings of ₹42,900 cr at a 15% margin) gives ₹2,564 a share, against a price of ₹1,568 and broker consensus near ₹2,296. A 12x multiple is itself a perpetual-growth assumption: at our cost of equity it implies growth of 8.8%. Godrej does not disclose an embedded margin, so we test 12–18% margins and 10–14x multiples: value ranges from ₹1,709 to ₹3,589, and only the harshest combination falls below the price.

The latest quarter (Q2 FY27) sharpens each story. DLF booked ₹3,800 cr, taking H1 to ₹4,457 cr against ₹20,000 cr full-year guidance, so the year now rests on second-half launches, a real execution risk; net cash rose to ₹15,100 cr. Godrej's H1 bookings rose 7% to ₹16,659 cr and collections 25% to ₹9,666 cr, with H1 operating cash flow of ₹1,983 cr, so cash conversion, the risk we flagged, is improving. Phoenix's retail rental income grew 16% in H1 and management guides 14–15% for FY27–28, almost exactly our DCF path. Against its own recent history the sector looks reasonable. The Nifty Realty index trades at about 40x earnings against a five-year median near 48x. But those five years were the up-cycle; measured against intrinsic value rather than recent multiples, the premium remains.

What management says: transcripts, guidance and BRSR

We read the companies' own earnings-call transcripts (DLF and Phoenix Q4 FY26; Godrej Q2 FY27) and sustainability filings (DLF BRSR FY25, Godrej BRSR FY26, DCCDL Sustainability Report FY26). Three findings change how our numbers should be read. First, DLF's headline net cash is mostly not free: management said about ₹11,200 cr of the ₹14,155 cr sits in RERA escrow accounts, released only as projects complete in FY27–28. Second, DLF guides to ₹9,000–10,000 cr of new margin and ₹7,000–8,000 cr of development free cash flow every year. Recognised revenue, booked only on completion, reports years late. Capitalising management's own ₹7,500 cr at our cost of equity, growing 5% a year, gives a development business worth ₹88,690 cr; adding DCCDL and free net cash gives ₹452 a share, or ₹551 with DCCDL at the 7.5% cap rate. Both are below the ₹692 price. On management's numbers, the market is paying for land beyond any guided horizon. Third, guidance has been credible: DLF met its FY26 bookings guide (101%) and Godrej beat its own (105%), though Godrej missed its collections target (95%) and DLF's H1 FY27 run-rate is only 45% of pro-rata guidance.

Phoenix's call supports our forecast: retail rental growth guided at 14–15% for FY27–28, with 36–50% of leased area renewing in two to three years at about 20% uplifts; offices are 70% occupied (EBITDA ₹141 cr) and hotels earned ₹276 cr. On ESG, DLF used 1.20 million GJ in FY25, 16% renewable (up from 14%); women are only 10% of employees and their turnover (35%) is almost twice men's, a weak point on the social side. DCCDL's emissions were flat at about 304 ktCO2e while rent grew about 15%, and its whole rental portfolio is LEED Platinum. That matters for value: green Grade-A offices are what multinational tenants and global capital (GIC, CPPIB) require, and they explain DCCDL's 98–99% non-SEZ occupancy. Godrej's FY26 BRSR is the contrast: women are 38% of permanent staff (DLF 10%), renewables 33% of energy (15% a year earlier), zero lost-time injuries. Its reporting boundary covers only its own sites and excludes contractors, so the energy totals cannot be compared with DLF's. Phoenix's filings could not be retrieved; we did not use second-hand ESG figures for it.

The sell-side sits above the market: median targets are ₹752 for DLF, ₹2,350 for Godrej and ₹2,200 for Phoenix, against prices of ₹692, ₹1,568 and ₹1,957. The difference from our DCF is not arithmetic; it is the required return (asset-level 11.75–12% and cap rates of 6.5–8%, against CAPM rates of 13–15%) and the willingness to pay for land and growth beyond any explicit forecast. Brokers and our model agree on the ranking: Godrej offers the most upside, DLF is roughly fair, Phoenix is fully priced (H9).

10Through time: announcements, shocks, survivors and REITs

Event studies · market efficiency · survivorship · REIT instruments · value of delay

Finally we tested our conclusions against history: how the shares reacted to shocks and company news, what happened to developers that failed, and what delays cost.

A valuation at one date hides how the market has priced news in the past. We ran market-model event studies on daily prices since 2007: abnormal return = actual return less the return the stock's beta against the Nifty predicts, estimated over the year before each event and summed over three days.

Shocks hit hard but briefly. Demonetisation cost DLF -15% in three days, the COVID lockdown -16%, the IL&FS credit freeze cost Godrej -9%; yet a year later DLF was 26% and 46% ahead of the Nifty and Godrej 62% ahead (H11). The market was repricing timing; franchise value was intact. REIT listings barely registered (|CAR| ≤ 4%, none significant): the market already knew rental assets could be monetised. Rate decisions moved prices less than the rate-sensitivity debate suggests, and cuts were often 'sold on the news'. Single-event figures can be contaminated by same-week company news (Godrej's jump at RERA's start, Phoenix's fall in late August 2020), so we rely on averages and t-statistics.

Company news tells a sharper story. Asset monetisations (GIC's purchase of a third of DCCDL in 2017, Phoenix's GIC platform in 2020–21 and its CPPIB buy-out in 2025) earned 5.4% on average (t 2.3): investors reward evidence that private capital values rental assets above book. DLF's sell-out launches averaged 2.8% (The Arbour, ₹8,000 cr in three days: +7.4%, t 3.0). Results days are different: across 179 results announcements since 2008, the EPS surprise explains only 0%–15% of the price move and gets its direction right about half the time (H10). Under completion accounting, profit is old news; bookings and launches are the information. This is the strongest evidence for valuing developers on cash and pre-sales rather than earnings. Equity issues show the opposite pattern: the five equity-issue announcements followed run-ups of 7.3%, mostly near the top of their three-year range (Godrej's three QIPs at the 85th–100th percentile), and were followed by a drift of -8.0% over sixty days (H12). Godrej's ₹6,000 cr QIP of November 2024 preceded a 33% fall relative to the Nifty.

Survivorship and delivery. A sector index built from today's listed names flatters history. An equal-weighted basket of nine survivors returned 454% since January 2008; six developers that failed or stalled (Unitech, HDIL, Parsvnath, Ansal, Omaxe, Peninsula) lost 92%, four of them more than 99%. Their betas were higher (Unitech 2.65, HDIL 2.56), so excluding them biases the industry beta down: our lens-A beta is best read as a floor. Between demonetisation and COVID, quality developers rose 78% while the stressed basket fell 66%: RERA, escrow and the credit freeze turned on-time delivery into a moat. Delay has a price even for survivors. If every cash flow slips a year, value falls by V × r/(1+r): ₹21 a share for DLF on our DCF, ₹111 for Godrej, ₹60 for Phoenix; on management's own cash-flow case DLF loses ₹46 a share per year of slippage. With H1 FY27 bookings at 45% of pro-rata guidance, that is a live risk.

REITs as instruments. Listed REITs distribute at least 90% of cash and now yield 6.1% against a G-Sec of 7.4%, a spread of -1.3%, averaging -0.5% since 2020: investors accept less than the risk-free rate because distributions grow. Yet monthly changes in REIT yields do not explain developer returns (|t| < 1): DLF and Phoenix are priced as growth stocks rather than yield vehicles (H8). The REIT market is nonetheless Phoenix's exit option: at the mall REIT's yield its retail EBITDA is worth ₹38,588 cr, against ₹22,438 cr on our CAPM rate, 72% more. The CPPIB and GIC transactions show that option being exercised, and it explains why practitioner NAVs sit above our DCF.

11Loose ends

Module 9 · cross holdings, minority interests, distress, liquidity

Several technical items needed a decision before the final numbers. This section records how we handled each.

  • Cross holdings. DCCDL is valued separately and added to operating value rather than mixed into EBIT; consolidating it would double-count.
  • Minority interests. CPPIB and GIC own stakes in Phoenix's mall platforms; we deduct their share of value in proportion to their share of profit (about 21%). Phoenix is buying out CPPIB's 49% of Island Star Mall Developers for about ₹5,450 cr in four tranches over three years (its stake has reached 68.1%). Bought at a fair price this is value-neutral — cash out, minority leakage down — so we keep the FY26 minority share.
  • Distress. Godrej's original Altman Z is in the distress zone and its cash cover is 2.1x; we add an expected distress cost in the APV check.
  • Liquidity and control. All three are liquid: average daily traded value over the past year ₹238 cr (DLF), ₹160 cr (Godrej), ₹95 cr (Phoenix), with Amihud illiquidity a fraction of small peers such as Sobha and of the Nexus REIT, so no illiquidity discount applies; no control premium is relevant to a minority investor.

12Synthesis: what you have to believe

The methods disagree, and the disagreement is the finding. Relative valuation compares each stock with peers that are also expensive, so it cannot see a sector-wide premium; intrinsic valuation can. To own these stocks at today's prices you have to believe one of three things: that their cash flows will grow with India's economy for ever; that the right long-run cost of equity for Indian real estate is close to the government bond yield; or that land banks hold option value our cash-flow model does not capture. Each is defensible for some investors. None is a margin of safety.

Recommendation (relative, within the sector)

Godrej Properties: preferred. Multiples slightly below the peer median and the lowest of our three; the highest chance in our simulation of value exceeding price; valued above price on 12x embedded profit. Watch: collections vs ₹24,000 cr guidance, land spend, net debt.

DLF: neutral. Best balance sheet and a rental platform worth roughly its book value; fully priced for long-run growth. Watch: luxury demand, DCCDL monetisation.

Phoenix Mills: least preferred. Excellent assets priced at a 3.0% yield. Watch: rental growth on new malls, consumption, minority leakage.

Limitations

  • Four years of statements from one aggregated source, plus prices from 2007. DLF and Godrej tie to their audited FY26 annual reports; Phoenix's filings could not be retrieved, and some operating KPIs and broker views come from press reports of results and broker notes.
  • Two credible cost-of-capital lenses give materially different values, and we report both.
  • Growth and margin paths are judgements; the scenario grid, reverse DCF and Monte Carlo show how much each matters.
  • Book-value metrics are distorted by cost-based accounting for land and investment property.
  • Timing: the RBI's October 2026 rate hike and rising unsold inventory are the main near-term risks to the developers; our valuation does not depend on them, but the market prices will.

Appendix AFurther valuation routes

Residual income (Palepu). With ROE fading to 15% over ten years, abnormal-earnings value is ₹157 for DLF and ₹362 for Godrej under lens A. The justified P/B on current ROE is 0.49 for DLF against a market P/B of 3.76; put the other way, DLF's price implies a sustained ROE of 41%. Part of this gap is accounting: land bought decades ago sits in book value at cost, so book understates economic capital and ROE overstates economic returns (H7). For developers, net asset value is a better anchor than book. Palepu's abnormal-earnings-growth model, which starts from earnings rather than book, lands in the same place (₹157 for DLF, ₹388 for Godrej, ₹356 for Phoenix): the price capitalises next year's earnings at 36x, 23x and 54x against a no-growth multiple (1/Ke) of about 6–8x.

DDM. A two-stage dividend model, using dividends declared in the last twelve months, gives ₹179 for DLF, ₹449 for Godrej and ₹359 for Phoenix. Godrej paid nothing from FY17 to FY26 and resumed with ₹10 a share in FY27; DLF's dividend has compounded about 4% a year since FY16. Payouts are low, so dividends capture little of the value. Real options. Undeveloped land is a call option on developed property (Damodaran). An illustrative land bank worth ₹90,000 cr developed, costing ₹1,00,000 cr to build (negative NPV today), is still worth ₹6,756 cr as an option at 30% volatility over ten years. DLF's market value exceeds our DCF equity value by ₹100,072 cr; land-bank optionality is one candidate explanation.

Appendix BInterest-rate sensitivity

Beyond CAPM. A two-factor model adds the monthly change in the G-Sec. Developers lose about 5%–6% per +100bp after controlling for the market, but with t-statistics of only -1.2 to -1.4; Phoenix shows no rate exposure. Rate risk is real but noisy (H4), so CAPM remains our base.
